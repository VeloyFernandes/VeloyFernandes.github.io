Icing on wind turbine airfoils

In a cold cloud, supercooled droplets too heavy to follow the air around a blade strike its leading edge and freeze on contact. This page grows that ice on a NACA 2412 section in your browser, from the flow field up, and re-reads the CFD and wind-tunnel results of a 2024 group project at Aalborg University that I worked on.

Where the water lands

The collection efficiency is the share of the oncoming water that reaches each point of the surface. It peaks near the stagnation point and falls to zero at the impingement limits, beyond which droplets are swept past. These charts follow the simulator.

The 2024 measurements, re-read

The project compared Ansys Fluent simulations with tests of 3D-printed clean and iced models in the open wind tunnel at AAU Esbjerg: 9 cm chord, 25 cm span, 20 m/s. The ice shapes came from Ansys FENSAP-ICE. The lines and points below are the project's own data; the dashed line is the boundary-layer model on this page.

The clean section: finite span, not the turbulence model

The tunnel model measured a lift slope of 0.067 per degree where the simulation gives 0.097. The simulated domain is an extruded section with no tips. The model is a wing of aspect ratio 2.8 whose tips sit under 3 cm from the tunnel walls, and lifting-line theory puts a free wing of that shape at 0.059. The measurement lies between the two, as it should. The same tip vortices add induced drag, which the simulation has none of and which accounts for the higher measured drag at high angles.

Drag at zero angle: transition matters

At a Reynolds number of 120,000 much of the boundary layer is laminar. The SST k-ω simulation treats it as turbulent from the nose and gives a drag coefficient of 0.0214. The tunnel measured 0.0139. The boundary-layer model here, which lets the flow stay laminar until it trips, gives .

The ice penalty: simulation and tunnel disagree

The simulation predicts a lift loss growing from 9% to 48% and a stall at 8°. The tunnel shows the opposite: 35% at 0°, then 4% and 3%, with no stall. The 0° figure rests on a lift coefficient of 0.11 against 0.16, a difference that a mounting error of 0.9° would produce, and each iced model was a separate print. The drag penalty is the firmer result: 11%, 29% and 34% in the tunnel.

What the project got right

Ice lowers lift and raises drag at every angle in both data sets. The icing run itself holds up: the ice mass from FENSAP-ICE is reproduced here from first principles to within a few percent.

Method

Flow. A Hess-Smith panel method: source panels on the surface plus one vortex strength fixed by the Kutta condition. It gives the velocity anywhere around the section, clean or iced, in milliseconds.

Droplets. Each droplet is released four chords upstream and integrated through the flow with the Schiller-Naumann drag law. How far it deviates from the air depends on the inertia parameter K, which grows with droplet size and speed and falls with chord. The spacing of the droplets where they land, compared with where they started, is the collection efficiency.

Ice. Rime: every droplet freezes where it lands, so the ice thickness is the water caught divided by the ice density, 917 kg/m³. The surface moves along its normal, layer by layer, with the flow and trajectories recomputed each time. The grown area is matched to the water caught, so mass is conserved.

Drag. Thwaites' method for the laminar boundary layer, Michel's criterion for transition, Head's method for the turbulent part and the Squire-Young formula. On the iced section the boundary layer is tripped at the edge of the ice.

Limits. The rime rule holds for cold, dry icing. Near freezing, water runs back before it freezes and forms glaze horns, which this model does not grow. The flow is ideal and the boundary layer does not feed back on it, so separation behind the ice, ice roughness and stall are not captured: the drag figure for the iced section is a lower bound. One droplet size, no gravity, two dimensions.

Code. The same models are in Python with tests in the GitHub repository. The Python and browser versions agree to within 2%.
